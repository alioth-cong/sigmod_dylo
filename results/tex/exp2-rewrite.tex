\stitle{Exp-2: Where the efficiency and the cost saving come from.}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} separates two questions that the same table is often
asked to answer at once: which components make \DyLo{} fast, and which make it
cheap. The first three variants remove the implementation techniques of
Section~\ref{sec:impl}. Certificates ($V_1$) and incremental cost-tree
maintenance ($V_2$) are \emph{exactly} neutral: removing either leaves the
committed set bit-identical on every batch of all three streams and leaves
$\mathsf C(D)$ unchanged to three decimals, while per-batch latency rises by
$1.6$--$2.4\times$ and $1.3$--$2.1\times$ respectively. They buy time and
nothing else, which is what a sound implementation optimization should do.
Candidate pruning ($V_3$) is the one technique that is not neutral: it is a
deliberate speed/quality trade, and removing it slows the system by
$1.4$--$1.6\times$ while \emph{raising} $\mathsf C(D)$ by $6.1$--$8.6\%$,
because the unpruned selector admits moves whose ancestor closures are more
expensive to refresh.

The last variant removes a contribution rather than an optimization. $V_4$
disables the accrual ledger, so a move is priced by its destination alone and
the deferred source's amortized share is never charged. This is the largest
single effect in the table: the committed set changes on $41/41$, $320/321$ and
$284/300$ batches, the selector admits $56\%$, $18\%$ and $3.8\%$ more moves,
and $\mathsf C(D)$ rises by $17.3\%$, $24.2\%$ and $6.3\%$. The cost advantage
of Section~\ref{sec:algo} is therefore attributable to a component we can switch
off, not merely consistent with a theorem.

\emph{The surrogate is sufficient, not just tractable.} Algorithm~\ref{alg:main}
guards each committed round with a realized-$\Delta F$ check and repairs a
rejected round by peeling. Across eight graphs and three instances the check
rejected \emph{none} of the $1{,}437$ non-empty sets the surrogate proposed and
the repair never fired, so on the evaluated suite solving the surrogate exactly
also solves the objective it stands in for. Two graphs (Facebook, Twitter)
contribute no rounds at $\lambda=10^{-1}$ and are excluded from that count.

\emph{Exactness of the selector is not separately observable here.} Replacing
\textsc{OpenSet} with a per-destination greedy that charges each accepted move
its full ancestor closure yields a bit-identical committed set on all three
graphs at $\lambda=10^{-1}$. The two selectors diverge only for
$\lambda\in[10^{-3},3\times10^{-3}]$, where the exact program admits sets a
greedy rejects and attains a $32$--$100\%$ higher objective; outside that window
the affected region per round is too sparse for sibling subtrees to share an
ancestor, and the two agree. We therefore present the tree program as the
mechanism that makes the exact selector linear-time rather than as an
independent source of the measured savings.

\emph{Dispersion.} \DyLo{} is deterministic given its input and detector seed.
To bound the effect of detector randomness we repeated all six graphs and three
instances under five seeds: across the eighteen combinations the range of $Q$ is
at most $3.3\times10^{-6}$ and of $\mathsf C(D)$ at most $1.0\%$, two orders of
magnitude below the differences on which Table~\ref{tab:main} separates methods.
Per-batch latency is noisier (up to $48\%$ range on the smallest graph, under
$5\%$ on the largest), as expected for sub-millisecond timings.
