\stitle{Exp-8: Fewer tokens at equal answer quality.}
\DyLo{} regenerates $394$ of the $1{,}465$ communities it maintains and spends
$2.51$M input tokens on the ingested batch, against $3.62$--$5.49$M for the
alternatives---$54.2\%$ below a full rebuild and $30.5\%$ below the cheapest
incremental baseline. What this experiment asks is whether that saving costs
anything, so it tests \emph{non-inferiority} rather than superiority, against a
margin fixed in advance at $\delta=0.201$ in Bradley--Terry units, the difference
corresponding to a $45\%$ win rate.

Both confounds a reader would raise are removed by construction rather than by
adjustment. \DyLo{} maintains a finer top of the hierarchy and would otherwise
expose $558$ level-$\le1$ reports against $453$--$486$ for the others, so every
arm is randomly downsampled to $453$ under a fixed seed; and because the judges
reward length strongly---a length covariate carries a coefficient of $+4.76$ and
$+5.84$, larger than any method effect in the study---all eight arms answer under
one shared response template capping the answer at $250$ words, which brings
\DyLo{}'s median answer from $17.4\%$ longer than the longest baseline to
$9.1\%$. Retrieval budget needed no intervention: \DyLo{} already reads $5.10$
summaries per query against \QCA{}'s $5.14$.

Under those conditions the point estimate favours \DyLo{} against all seven
alternatives under both judges ($+0.12$ to $+0.47$), and after Holm--Bonferroni
correction across the seven tests the non-inferiority margin is cleared in seven
of seven comparisons under GPT-5.6~Sol and five of seven under Opus~5. The two
exceptions---against a rebuild and DF-\Louvain{}, both at $p_{\text{H}}=.067$---have
positive point estimates ($+0.20$, $+0.12$) with intervals too wide to resolve at
$76$ questions; they are inconclusive rather than adverse. On the full $120$
questions, where arms that fail to answer are not excluded, the comparison is
stronger: \DyLo{} leads both judges ($+0.497$ $[+0.281,+0.716]$ and $+0.567$
$[+0.336,+0.816]$) and wins all seven pairwise comparisons after Holm correction
applied across the full family of $\binom{8}{2}=28$ pairs, at win rates of
$60.4$--$70.8\%$ and $63.6$--$69.0\%$. We treat the matched subset as the primary
result because it is the conservative one, and note that the $28$-pair family
makes the full-set claim conservative in turn.

\stitle{Why the coverage gap appears, and what it is.}
The gap between the two views is itself informative. \DyLo{} produces a
substantive answer on $117$ of $120$ questions; the baselines manage $98$--$110$,
and on the $44$ questions excluded above at least one arm returns GraphRAG's
refusal string rather than an answer. This is not a retrieval-budget effect---the
budgets are equal---but a consequence of what cost awareness does to community
size. Because $w(c)$ counts members, a large community is expensive to dirty and
the selector avoids it: \DyLo{}'s largest level-$\le1$ community holds $209$
entities against $282$--$707$ for the baselines, while the medians are
indistinguishable ($10$--$13$). A summary spanning seven hundred entities is too
general for map--reduce retrieval to extract specific evidence from, and the arm
falls back to a refusal. Cost awareness thus improves answerability as a side
effect of the objective it optimizes; we report that separately from the quality
claim rather than folding it in.
